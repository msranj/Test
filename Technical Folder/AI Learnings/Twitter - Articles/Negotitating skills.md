

# How to Negotiate (Almost) Anything — Trainer's Notes

*A condensed, practice-ready guide. Focus: what actually moves outcomes, not clever phrases.*

---

## The One Idea That Matters Most

**Your leverage comes from your walk-away option, not your persuasion skills.** Everything below builds on this. A weak talker with a strong BATNA beats a smooth talker with no alternative, every time.

---

## PART 1 — Prepare Before You Speak

### 1. Build Your BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement)
- List every fallback if this deal falls through. Turn the two best into *real* options (get actual quotes/offers) before you sit down.
- Even an uncertain or imagined alternative boosts your confidence and results — but never bluff an option you don't have; one challenge collapses your credibility.
- **Action:** Before any negotiation, ask "What will I do if this fails?" and make that answer concrete.

### 2. Set Three Numbers, in Writing
| Number | What it does | Example (freelance rate) |
|---|---|---|
| **Aspiration** | Best defensible outcome (market data/precedent) | $140/hr |
| **Realistic target** | What you expect to land near | $120/hr |
| **Reservation value** | Your walk-away floor (BATNA translated into deal terms) | $95/hr |
- Write these down *before* the conversation. Once a persuasive person is across the table, any concession starts sounding reasonable — your written floor protects you.

### 3. Estimate *Their* BATNA and Floor Too
- The overlap between your floor and theirs = **ZOPA** (Zone of Possible Agreement) — where a deal can actually happen.
- Ask indirectly: *"What happens on your side if we can't agree?"* Watch for deadline pressure, budget cycles, alternative vendors.
- Hold this estimate loosely — it's a theory, not a fact. Being wrong costs more than admitting uncertainty.

### 4. Map Interests, Not Just Positions
- Their **position** = what they're asking for. Their **interest** = *why*. The gap often contains the solution.
- E.g., a stalled price fight may really be about a budget rule, a manager's bonus target, or cash-flow timing — not the number itself.
- Ask who else is involved: decision-maker, hidden influencers, approval chain.

### 5. Decide What to Share vs. Protect
- **Green list** (share freely): goals, values, non-sensitive constraints — builds trust, surfaces win-win trades.
- **Red list** (protect): your true floor, urgent deadlines, desperation.
- **Question list**: what you still need to learn from them.
- Rule: protecting your floor is fine; lying about material facts is not.

### 6. Put It All on One Page
- 15–30 minutes for big stakes, 5 minutes when rushed. Capture: BATNA, aspiration/target/reservation, their likely BATNA, key interests, questions, possible trades, planned opening move.
- Treat it as a hypothesis you can update — not a script you're locked into.

## PART 2 — Opening and Running the Conversation

### 7. Set the Tone First
- Open warm, slow, unhurried *before* discussing terms — this makes both sides think more clearly, not just "nicer."
- Must be genuine; a rehearsed-sounding opener reads as manipulation. In more formal/high-context cultures, dial back familiarity.

### 8. Decide Who Opens — Your Info Advantage Is the Deciding Factor
- **You know the terrain well (comparables, economics)** → make the first offer. First offers anchor the final outcome.
- **They know more than you** → ask questions first, let them anchor, or gather reference points before naming any number.
- Rule of thumb: informed → anchor; uninformed → discover first, then decide.

### 9. If You Anchor, Anchor Ambitiously — With a Reason
- Open at the most favorable number you can *justify* (comparables, expected returns, cost logic) — not a number pulled from thin air.
- Precise numbers ($9,850 vs. $10,000) read as calculated, not guessed — but only if you can back it up.
- Caution: overly aggressive anchors raise the risk of *no deal*, especially with strangers or repeat relationships.

### 10. Use an "Accusation Audit" Sparingly
- Name the harshest objection yourself before they raise it: *"You may think this fee is high, that we're just another vendor..."*
- Signals self-awareness, lowers defensiveness. Overuse it and you plant doubts that weren't there — a scalpel, not a hammer.

### 11. Test Whether Price Is the Real Issue
- Ask: *"What makes this difficult on your side?"* or *"If price weren't the issue, what would we be discussing?"*
- Resistance that money alone can't explain often points to risk, control, status, fairness, or cash flow. Solve the real problem — but don't assume every disagreement hides one; sometimes price is just price.

## Quick Reference: What's Proven vs. What's Myth

| ✅ Backed by evidence | ⚠️ Use with caution / weak evidence |
|---|---|
| Strong BATNA = real power | "That's right" trick (no direct evidence it works) |
| Ambitious first offer (when informed) | Questions engineered to get a "no" |
| Perspective-taking (understanding their thinking) | Reading lies from body language (~54% accuracy — near chance) |
| Silence / 3+ second pauses | Accusation audits (practitioner-based, not tested) |
| Exchanging priority info to find trades | — |

**Key distinction:** *Perspective-taking* (analyzing their logic) beats *emotional empathy* (feeling their feelings) — empathy alone can make you over-accommodate.

---

## Trainer's Practice Checklist (Use Before Every Real Negotiation)

1. What's my BATNA — and is it real, not imagined?
2. What are my aspiration / target / reservation numbers, written down?
3. What's my best guess at their BATNA and floor?
4. What do they actually *want* (interest), not just ask for (position)?
5. What can I share freely vs. protect?
6. Do I have more information than them, or less? → decide who opens.
7. If I open: what's my ambitious, defensible number?
8. What's the real issue if price stalls the deal?
