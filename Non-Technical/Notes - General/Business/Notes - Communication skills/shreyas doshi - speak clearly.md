# How to Talk to Executives

## A practical guide to communicating competence, influence and impact

**Based on the video by Shreyas Doshi**

[Watch the original video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgFAKCEpEsE)

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. Executive summary

Being good at your job does not automatically give you a strong
professional reputation. Your work must be understood, recognized and
trusted by the people around you.

The speaker explains that competence becomes reputation through three
channels:

1.  **Your communication:** How clearly you explain your insights,
    execution and impact.
2.  **Your manager's advocacy:** Whether your manager understands your
    contribution and communicates it to others.
3.  **Your peers' perception:** Whether colleagues recognize your
    ability and value your work.

The central lesson is that communication is not an optional soft skill.
It is an important part of how your work is evaluated and how your
influence grows.

The speaker's practical advice is to understand how your organization
communicates, prepare deliberately for important meetings, speak at the
level of the audience, anticipate questions, focus on outcomes rather
than operational detail, remain concise and composed, follow up
carefully, and know your subject thoroughly.

## 2. How competence becomes reputation

Many professionals assume that if they consistently deliver good work,
people will naturally notice. The speaker describes this as a mistake he
made in his own career.

Competence and reputation are related, but they are not the same.

-   **Competence** is your ability to do the work well.
-   **Reputation** is what other people understand, believe and say
    about your ability and contribution.

The speaker identifies three ways competence is converted into
reputation.

### 2.1 Communicate your insight, execution and impact

Do not assume that people can see the value of your work simply because
the work has been completed.

Explain: - **Insight:** What did you discover or understand? -
**Execution:** What did you do? - **Impact:** What changed as a result?

The speaker emphasizes that communicating this is an active
responsibility. It does not happen automatically.

### 2.2 Build your manager's understanding and advocacy

Your manager should understand what you contributed, why it mattered and
what results it produced.

This does not mean repeatedly promoting yourself. It means communicating
progress, decisions, outcomes and challenges clearly enough that your
manager can accurately represent your work.

### 2.3 Develop a strong professional perception among peers

Your colleagues also form a view of your competence. Their perception is
influenced by the quality of your work, how you collaborate, how you
explain problems and whether they can rely on you.

**Key takeaway:** Delivering results is essential, but making those
results understandable is also part of the job.

## 3. Understand how your organization communicates

Every organization has a preferred way of sharing information and making
decisions.

Some organizations place greater importance on spoken presentations and
meetings. Others rely heavily on written documents.

The speaker uses Amazon and Stripe as examples of companies with strong
written communication cultures.

A person who is an excellent speaker but only an average writer may
struggle to influence decisions in a company where written documents
carry significant weight. The problem may not be a lack of competence.
It may be a mismatch between the person's strongest communication method
and the organization's decision-making culture.

### What you should do

Before trying to influence people, understand:

-   Are important decisions made through presentations, written
    documents or meetings?
-   Do leaders expect detailed written proposals before discussions?
-   Are decisions made in group meetings or through documents circulated
    in advance?
-   What level of writing or presentation quality is expected from
    influential people?

### Practical application

If your organization expects written proposals, improve your ability to
write clear problem statements, options, recommendations and business
justifications.

If leaders make decisions in review meetings, learn to present the issue
and recommendation clearly, and prepare for questions.

**Key takeaway:** Adapt your communication to the way your organization
works. Do not rely only on the communication style that feels most
comfortable to you.

## 4. Prepare more than usual for important meetings

The speaker strongly recommends allocating extra time to high-impact
communication events, such as major product reviews and leadership
presentations.

A common mistake is to prepare for an important presentation while
continuing to treat every routine task as equally urgent.

For example, a product manager may have a major review on Thursday but
continue attending every routine meeting, responding immediately to
every message and managing the usual workload. The presentation then
receives only the time left over.

The speaker recommends doing the opposite: protect preparation time.

### How to apply this

Before an important meeting:

1.  Identify the meeting's objective and the decision required.
2.  Reserve sufficient preparation time.
3.  Reduce or reschedule low-priority activities where possible.
4.  Prepare the main message and supporting evidence.
5.  Anticipate questions and objections.
6.  Practise explaining the recommendation clearly.

The speaker makes a useful observation: communication may occupy only a
small portion of your working time, but it can account for a much larger
portion of how your contribution is judged.

**Key takeaway:** Treat important communication moments as significant
work, not as an activity to prepare for at the last minute.

## 5. Communicate at the audience's level

Effective communication is not about explaining everything you know. It
is about explaining what the audience needs to understand.

The speaker compares this with product design: a product should be
designed around users' needs, not around what the product team
personally prefers.

The same principle applies when speaking to executives.

### Understand the audience's priorities

Before presenting, consider:

-   What does this person need to achieve?
-   What decisions are they responsible for?
-   What risks concern them?
-   What outcome are they expecting?
-   What information will help them make a decision?

Your explanation should be shaped around these priorities.

### Simulate the meeting before it happens

The speaker describes practising important presentations with members of
his team.

They would review the presentation and predict: - What the CEO might
ask. - What the engineering leader might challenge. - Which assumptions
might be questioned. - What objections could arise. - Which points would
create confidence or concern.

He found that many of the predicted questions appeared in the actual
meeting.

This is not about guessing every question. It is about understanding the
people involved well enough to prepare for their likely concerns.

### A simple preparation exercise

Write down the names or roles of the key attendees. For each person,
ask:

1.  What is their main responsibility?
2.  What would make this proposal valuable to them?
3.  What could make them reject or delay it?
4.  What evidence would they expect?
5.  What decision do I need from them?

**Key takeaway:** Do not prepare only what you want to say. Prepare for
what the audience is likely to ask and needs to decide.

## 6. Focus on impact, not only execution details

One of the most important ideas in the video is that people at different
levels of an organization may look at the same work from different
perspectives.

The speaker describes three levels of product work:

1.  **Optics:** How the work or proposal is presented and perceived.
2.  **Execution:** How the work will be delivered.
3.  **Impact:** What meaningful result the work is expected to achieve.

In an executive review, the product manager may focus heavily on
execution while the CEO or senior leader is primarily interested in
impact.

### Example from the video

An executive asks how a proposed initiative will progress.

The product manager responds with details about: - A three-week schedule
adjustment. - A team member being on parental leave. - The April
delivery milestone. - A table in the appendix explaining the schedule.

These details may be accurate and important for delivery planning.
However, they do not answer the executive's main concern: what impact
will the launch create?

The executive may conclude that the presenter understands the delivery
plan but has not clearly connected it to the intended business result.

### A better way to structure the response

Start with the impact:

> "This launch is intended to reduce customer onboarding time and
> improve completion rates. We expect the change to address the largest
> drop-off point in the current process."

Then explain the execution:

> "To deliver this, we need three engineering changes. The current plan
> targets the April milestone."

Then mention constraints only when relevant:

> "One resource constraint may affect the schedule. We have identified
> an alternative allocation and will confirm the revised date by
> Friday."

This approach gives the executive the outcome first and the delivery
details second.

### Applying the same idea to technical work

Suppose you are presenting a SQL Server migration.

**Execution-focused explanation:**

"We need to migrate 18 databases, update the compatibility level,
validate SQL Agent jobs, change connection strings and complete
application testing. The current team has two resource constraints."

**Impact-focused explanation:**

"The migration will move the application to a supported SQL Server
platform, reduce operational risk and improve our ability to meet
recovery and availability requirements. We plan to complete it in three
stages, with application validation before each production cutover."

The execution details still matter. They should support the business
outcome rather than obscure it.

**Key takeaway:** Lead with why the work matters. Explain how it will be
delivered after the audience understands the intended result.

## 7. Be concise and composed

The speaker recommends being concise and dispassionate when
communicating with senior leaders.

In this context, *dispassionate* does not mean lacking commitment or
enthusiasm. It means communicating calmly, objectively and without
making the discussion unnecessarily emotional or dramatic.

### Avoid long explanations

When an executive asks a question, answer the question directly.

Avoid lengthy accounts of every event that led to the situation. Too
much background can hide the important point.

A useful response structure is:

1.  **Answer:** Give the direct answer.
2.  **Reason:** Explain the main reason.
3.  **Evidence:** Provide the most relevant supporting fact.
4.  **Next step:** State what will happen next, if applicable.

### Example

Instead of:

"We started investigating this last week. First, we checked the server,
then we spoke to the application team, and after that we found some
configuration differences. We also noticed..."

Say:

"The issue is caused by a connection-pool configuration mismatch. We
have identified the affected application servers and are applying the
correction. We expect to complete validation by 4 PM."

The second response is easier to understand and gives the audience the
information needed to assess the situation.

### Maintain a steady tone

The speaker also warns against unpredictable emotional reactions. Senior
leaders should be able to expect a professional and measured response,
including when the news is difficult.

**Key takeaway:** Be direct, calm and factual. Show commitment through
the quality of your thinking and actions rather than through dramatic
language.

## 8. Follow up exceptionally well

The speaker emphasizes that a meeting does not end when people leave the
room.

Good follow-up includes:

-   Sharing meeting notes.
-   Recording decisions and action items.
-   Identifying owners and due dates.
-   Providing answers to questions that could not be resolved during the
    meeting.
-   Closing the loop on commitments.

If an executive asks a question and you promise to return with an
answer, send a concise response once you have the information.

### Example follow-up

**Subject: Follow-up -- Database Migration Review**

-   **Decision:** Proceed with the migration assessment for the two
    application databases.
-   **Action:** Complete dependency validation.
-   **Owner:** DBA team.
-   **Target date:** Friday.
-   **Open question:** Confirm application downtime tolerance.
-   **Follow-up:** The DBA team will share the revised migration plan
    after application-team confirmation.

If you promised an answer to a specific question, answer it clearly
rather than sending a long status update.

**Key takeaway:** Reliable follow-up builds trust. It demonstrates that
you listen, take responsibility and complete what you commit to.

## 9. Know your subject. Do not bluff.

The speaker is particularly direct on this point: communication
techniques cannot compensate for a lack of knowledge.

He warns against pretending to understand something or giving answers
that are not supported by facts.

A person may sometimes get away with vague explanations in a familiar
team environment. The speaker cautions against assuming the same
approach will work with executives.

Senior leaders have often encountered similar situations many times.
They may recognize unsupported claims, evasive answers and gaps in
understanding.

### What to do when you do not know the answer

Do not guess.

Instead:

1.  State what you know.
2.  Identify what is not yet confirmed.
3.  Explain how you will verify it.
4.  Give a realistic time for the follow-up.

### Example

An executive asks whether a database migration can be completed without
downtime.

A weak answer:

"Yes, it should be possible. We will manage it."

A more credible answer:

"We have not yet confirmed whether zero downtime is achievable. It
depends on application connection handling, replication or
synchronization options, and the cutover design. We will validate these
dependencies and present the available options with their downtime and
risk implications."

**Key takeaway:** Credibility comes from knowledge, evidence and
honesty. It is better to acknowledge an unknown than to provide a
confident but unsupported answer.

## 10. Treat communication as a discipline

The speaker notes that most communication principles are not complicated
or entirely new. People have usually heard some version of them before.

The difficulty is applying them consistently.

His advice is to select a few principles, commit to practising them and
develop the discipline to use them in real situations.

A practical starting set is:

-   Understand your organization's communication culture.
-   Protect preparation time for important meetings.
-   Think from the audience's perspective.
-   Lead with impact.
-   Keep answers concise and composed.
-   Follow up on every commitment.
-   Never bluff.

Improvement comes from repeated practice, not simply knowing the
principles.

## 11. Run your own performance-review cycle

Near the end of the supplied transcript, the speaker recommends that
people---especially those who are three to six months into a new
role---should consider running their own performance-review cycle
instead of waiting for the company's official review process.

He suggests this can be particularly useful when the working
relationship has been difficult.

### What can be taken from the available transcript

The clear recommendation is to seek feedback proactively and not depend
entirely on the organization's formal review calendar.

### Transcript limitation

The supplied transcript ends immediately after the speaker introduces
this idea. It does not contain the full explanation of how to run the
personal review cycle.

The following practical prompts are therefore suggestions for applying
the available idea, not a complete framework attributed to the speaker:

-   What outcomes have I delivered since joining?
-   What impact did those outcomes create?
-   What feedback have I received?
-   Where am I falling short of expectations?
-   What should I improve over the next review period?
-   What support or clarification do I need from my manager?

# 12. A practical executive communication framework

The following framework consolidates the video's recommendations into a
repeatable preparation method. It is a practical synthesis, not a named
framework from the speaker.

## Before the meeting

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Step                                What to prepare
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Understand the audience             Identify their responsibilities,
                                      priorities and likely concerns.

  Define the objective                Be clear about the decision,
                                      approval or support you need.

  Lead with impact                    Explain why the work matters to the
                                      organization.

  Prepare evidence                    Use relevant facts, metrics, risks
                                      and options.

  Anticipate questions                Consider objections, trade-offs and
                                      dependencies.

  Protect preparation time            Reduce routine work where possible
                                      before important meetings.
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

## During the meeting

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Step                                What to do
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Start with the outcome              State the business impact or main
                                      message first.

  Be concise                          Answer the question directly before
                                      adding detail.

  Stay composed                       Communicate calmly, especially when
                                      challenged.

  Explain execution                   Provide the plan, ownership and
                                      timeline.

  Be honest                           Clearly identify assumptions and
                                      unknowns.

  Confirm the decision                Make sure everyone understands the
                                      agreed next step.
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

## After the meeting

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Step                                What to do
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Share notes                         Record decisions and important
                                      discussion points.

  Assign actions                      Identify owners and due dates.

  Close open questions                Send promised information promptly.

  Track commitments                   Follow through until actions are
                                      completed.

  Review your performance             Identify what worked and what needs
                                      improvement.
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

# 13. Applying the principles to a SQL Server DBA role

The examples below apply the video's ideas to technical work. They are
illustrative applications, not examples quoted from the video.

## Scenario A: Reporting a production performance issue

**Technical explanation**

"The query is scanning a large table. We checked the execution plan,
statistics and indexes. We are reviewing the parameter values and the
query store history."

**Executive-level explanation**

"One business-critical application is experiencing slower response
times, affecting user transactions. We have isolated the database query
responsible and are validating a correction. We will share the expected
performance improvement and deployment risk before applying the
production change."

Why this works: - It explains the business impact first. - It gives
confidence that the issue is understood. - It avoids unnecessary
technical detail. - It identifies the next step.

## Scenario B: Requesting approval for a SQL Server migration

**Technical explanation**

"We need to migrate the databases, logins, SQL Agent jobs, linked
servers, SSIS packages and other dependencies. We also need to validate
compatibility and test the application."

**Executive-level explanation**

"The current SQL Server platform creates supportability and operational
risks. The proposed migration will move the application to a supported
platform and improve our ability to maintain availability and recovery
requirements. We need approval for the migration window and
application-testing resources."

Why this works: - It explains why the migration matters. - It connects
the technical work to risk reduction. - It makes the requested decision
clear.

## Scenario C: Explaining an infrastructure capacity issue

**Technical explanation**

"CPU utilization is high, memory pressure is increasing and storage
latency is above the normal baseline."

**Executive-level explanation**

"The database platform is approaching its current capacity during peak
business hours. If demand continues to increase, application response
times may deteriorate. We have assessed the available options: query
optimization, workload scheduling and additional infrastructure
capacity. We recommend starting with the changes that address the root
cause and provide the best cost-to-benefit outcome."

Why this works: - It communicates the risk in business terms. - It
presents options rather than only reporting symptoms. - It gives the
executive a basis for a decision.

# 14. Quick reference: the seven habits to practise

1.  **Make your contribution visible:** Communicate your insights,
    execution and impact.
2.  **Adapt to the organization:** Understand whether influence comes
    mainly through writing, presentations or meetings.
3.  **Prepare deliberately:** Allocate enough time for important
    communication events.
4.  **Think like the audience:** Anticipate what leaders care about and
    the questions they may ask.
5.  **Lead with impact:** Explain the outcome before diving into
    implementation details.
6.  **Communicate professionally:** Be concise, composed and accurate.
7.  **Close the loop:** Follow up on questions, decisions and
    commitments.

## Final takeaway

**Your work creates value, but your communication helps others
understand that value.**

Strong executive communication is not about using impressive language or
presenting every technical detail. It is about understanding what
matters to the audience, explaining the impact clearly, supporting your
message with evidence and following through on your commitments.

The goal is not merely to sound senior. It is to make your thinking,
contribution and judgment easy for decision-makers to understand and
trust.
